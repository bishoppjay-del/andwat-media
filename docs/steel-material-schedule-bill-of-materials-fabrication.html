Steel material schedule (BOM) for fabrication jobs in Australia: what to list and how to check it

Last reviewed 12 November 2026 · Sources linked below · Written by Jay at Andwat Co, an Australian boilermaker/fabricator (Cert III Engineering, 12+ years in the trade). About

How Australian fab shops build a steel material schedule or bill of materials (BOM) from the drawings: sections and grades, plate by kg/m², bolting categories (4.6/S, 8.8/S, 8.8/TB), coatings, AS/NZS 5131 test certificates and lot traceability, buy lengths, and a worked example.

A material schedule (a lot of shops call it the BOM, or bill of materials) is the list of everything a steel job needs: sections, plate, flat bar, bolts, anchors and coatings, with sizes, grades, quantities and weights. The takeoff measures the quantities off the drawings. The material schedule turns them into a supplier order, a cost and a record of what went into the job, which is what the builder asks for at handover. Here's what goes in one and how to check it, with figures from the Australian Steel Institute (ASI) and InfraBuild so you can check them yourself.

The short answer

  • One line per item: mark or group, description, size, grade, standard, quantity, unit, weight and drawing reference.
  • Sections and plate by kg/m and kg/m², bought in stock lengths and sheet sizes, not total metres.
  • Bolts by bolting category (4.6/S, 8.8/S, 8.8/TB or 8.8/TF), size and length, with nuts and washers.
  • Coatings by system and surface area or weight (galvanizing is usually priced by weight).
  • Certificates and traceability: note which items need mill test certificates and lot traceability for the job's Construction Category.
  • Revision control: the schedule carries the drawing revision it was built from.

Sections, grades and plate

Australian hot-rolled sections are named by size class, type and mass per metre: a 310UB40.4 weighs 40.4 kg/m. InfraBuild's Know Your Steel guide lists its universal beams and channels to AS/NZS 3679.1, hollow sections to AS/NZS 1163 (grades such as C350L0) and plate to AS/NZS 3678. The ASI's National Structural Steelwork Specification uses the same standards. Put the grade on every line: 300 and 350 grade, or C350L0 and C450L0 hollow sections, aren't interchangeable once the engineer has specified one.

For plate, mass is area x kg/m². At 7,850 kg/m³, steel is 7.85 kg/m² per millimetre of thickness, so 10 mm plate is 78.5 kg/m² (the figure InfraBuild lists) and 12 mm is 94.2 kg/m². List plate parts (base plates, cleats, gussets, stiffeners) individually, then add a line for the sheet you'll actually buy. The steel weight formulas cover flat bar, round bar and hollow sections.

Bolts, anchors and fasteners

Bolts are where BOMs most often come up short. AS 4100 bolting categories tell you both the bolt and how it's tightened. The ASI's bolt types and categories note summarises them:

  • 4.6/S: commercial Property Class 4.6 bolts to AS 1111, snug-tightened.
  • 8.8/S: Property Class 8.8 bolts, snug-tightened. The ASI calls it the most common category for flexible connections in Australia, normally high-strength structural bolts to AS/NZS 1252.
  • 8.8/TB and 8.8/TF: high-strength structural bolts to AS/NZS 1252, fully tensioned to AS 4100 (bearing-type and friction-type joints). The ASI notes 8.8/TB is the most common in rigid connections, and that the cost of tensioning matters.

List each line as size x length + category (for example M20 x 60 8.8/S, galv) with the count of bolts, nuts and washers. Count them per connection and multiply by how many times each "typical" connection occurs. Add chemical or cast-in anchors and their embedment from the drawings, plus a few spares.

Coatings

Note the coating system the specification calls up. Hot-dip galvanizing is normally to AS/NZS 4680 and is usually priced by the tonne, so your BOM weight drives the galv quote. Paint systems (often specified from AS/NZS 2312.1) are priced by surface area and coats. Check vent and drain holes are on the drawings for galvanized hollow sections, and allow for them in the shop hours.

Mill certificates and traceability (AS/NZS 5131)

On jobs fabricated to AS/NZS 5131, the material schedule is also your traceability record. The ASI's AS/NZS 5131 update and Tech Note TN017 set out the baseline for each Construction Category (CC):

  • CC1: test certificates available for all steel, and the grade stays identifiable and can be matched to the fabrication drawings.
  • CC2: test certificates provided for all steel, with lot traceability for main members, the connections between them and major plate components.
  • CC3 and CC4: test certificates for all steel and lot traceability for all items, including cleats and brackets. Piece-mark or piece traceability only if the construction specification calls for it.

TN017 notes that most structures in Australia should be CC2, and that material identification has to be transferred when part material goes back into stock. In practice: add a heat number and certificate column, fill it in when steel arrives, and transfer the marking onto offcuts before they go back on the rack. Those records then feed your ITP and handover pack.

Check it before you order

  1. Is it built off the latest drawing revision, and does it say which one?
  2. Does every mark on the drawings appear, with typical details multiplied out?
  3. Does every line have a grade and standard, not just a size?
  4. Are sections and plate ordered as whole lengths and sheets, with kerf and trim allowed? (See the cut list calculator guide.)
  5. Are bolts listed by category, with nuts, washers, anchors and spares?
  6. Is the coating system and the galv weight or paint area in?
  7. Is there a column for heat numbers and certificates, if the job is CC2 or higher?

Common questions

What is a material schedule in steel fabrication?

It's the list of everything you have to buy or pull from stock for a job: every section, plate, flat bar, bolt, anchor and coating, with sizes, grades, quantities and weights. The takeoff measures quantities off the drawings; the material schedule turns them into an order and a record of what went into the job.

Is a material schedule the same as a cut list?

No. The cut list is the saw list of every piece and length. The material schedule (or bill of materials) is the buy and record list: stock lengths, plate sheets, fasteners and coatings, plus grades and certificates. A good takeoff feeds both.

Do I need mill certificates for every piece of steel?

On AS/NZS 5131 work, test certificates have to be available (CC1) or provided (CC2 to CC4) for all steel material. Lot traceability is the baseline for CC2, CC3 and CC4, and covers more components as the category goes up. Piece-mark or piece traceability only applies on CC3 and CC4 if the construction specification calls for it, according to the Australian Steel Institute.

How do I list bolts on a steel BOM?

By size, length, property class and bolting category, for example M20 x 60 8.8/S. The Australian Steel Institute describes 4.6/S as commercial bolts to AS 1111 snug-tightened, and 8.8/S, 8.8/TB and 8.8/TF as Property Class 8.8 high-strength structural bolts to AS/NZS 1252, either snug-tightened or fully tensioned. Count nuts and washers as part of the assembly.

How do I work out plate weight for a BOM?

Area in square metres times the mass per square metre for the thickness. Steel at 7,850 kg/m³ gives 7.85 kg/m² per millimetre of thickness, so 10 mm plate is 78.5 kg/m² and 12 mm is 94.2 kg/m². A 250 x 250 x 12 base plate is 0.0625 m² x 94.2 = about 5.9 kg.
