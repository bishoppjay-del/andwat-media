Welding fume exposure limits: what changes on 1 December 2026 (AU fab shops)

Australia swaps the workplace exposure standards (WES) for workplace exposure limits (WEL) on 1 December 2026. The 1 mg/m³ welding fume limit, what changes for zinc (galv), manganese, nickel and chromium VI, Queensland's 2029 transition, air monitoring and records.

If you weld, cut or gouge for a living, the airborne contaminant rules get a rename and a refresh on 1 December 2026. Safe Work Australia's Workplace exposure standards (WES) list is replaced by the Workplace exposure limits (WEL) list. The headline welding fume number doesn't move, but a few of the metals in the fume do, and Queensland has its own transition for some of them. Here's what a fab shop or mobile welder actually needs to know.

The short answer

  • Welding fumes (not otherwise classified): 1 mg/m³ as an 8-hour time-weighted average (TWA). That was cut from 5 mg/m³ in January 2024 and stays at 1 mg/m³ in the WEL list.
  • From 1 December 2026 the WEL list is the reference. Until 30 November 2026 you still have to stay under the WES list.
  • You have to keep exposure under the limit for the total fume and for each component that has its own limit (manganese, zinc oxide, nickel and so on), not just the total.
  • Galvanised work: zinc oxide drops from 5 mg/m³ (fume) to 2 mg/m³ TWA. It isn't on Queensland's transition list, so in QLD that applies from 1 December 2026.
  • Stainless: chromium VI compounds lose their numeric limit (0.05 mg/m³ under the WES). They're listed as non-threshold genotoxic carcinogens, so the duty is to eliminate or minimise exposure as far as reasonably practicable.
  • Queensland: manganese and nickel are among 15 substances that keep their existing WES until 1 December 2029.

All values are mg/m³ as an 8-hour TWA unless marked. WES values are from the November 2025 WES list; WEL values are from the November 2026 WEL list. Check the source lists before you rely on a number.

Why the component limits bite first: a high-tensile or hardfacing job can put out a lot of manganese, and galv puts out zinc oxide. You can be under 1 mg/m³ total fume and still be over a component limit. The model Code of Practice: Welding processes says to consider total fume concentrations as well as the individual components.

What the law actually says (Queensland)

  • Section 49, WHS Regulation 2011 (Qld): a PCBU must ensure no person at the workplace is exposed to a substance or mixture in an airborne concentration that exceeds the exposure standard. Maximum penalty: 60 penalty units.
  • Section 50: air monitoring must be carried out if you're not certain on reasonable grounds whether the concentration exceeds the exposure standard, or if monitoring is needed to work out whether there's a risk to health. Results must be recorded and kept for 30 years and be readily accessible to anyone who may be exposed.
  • The primary duty to eliminate risks, or minimise them so far as is reasonably practicable, applies whether or not a substance has a number attached.

Safe Work Australia's WEL document says that from the end of the transition the model regulations require PCBUs to keep exposure under the WEL and to do air monitoring if they aren't sure. Queensland's reg text currently says "exposure standard"; WorkSafe Queensland confirms that most WELs apply in Queensland from 1 December 2026.

Air monitoring: how it's done

  • To compare against a limit, samples are taken in the worker's breathing zone, the hemisphere in front of the face within 30 cm of the nose. For a welder that usually means behind the helmet.
  • The welding code says monitoring should be done by a competent person such as an occupational hygienist, and should also cover other workers nearby.
  • Use the results to check your extraction actually works. If they show controls aren't working, review and fix them.
  • Unless the WEL list says otherwise, particulate limits apply to the inhalable fraction. Manganese now has a separate respirable limit as well.

Controls that actually reduce fume

From the model Code of Practice: Welding processes and WorkSafe Queensland, in order of preference:

  • Eliminate or reduce at the source: remove paint, oil and coatings before welding, pick lower-fume processes and consumables, and check the consumable's SDS for what it gives off.
  • Isolate: weld in booths or screened bays away from other workers.
  • Local exhaust ventilation (LEV): the code says LEV should give a minimum capture velocity of 0.5 m/s at the fume source, keep inlets clear, and not recirculate air into the workroom. Options include down-draught or side-draught benches, movable hoods on flexible ducts, and on-gun extraction.
  • Admin controls: safe work procedures, rotating hot jobs, keeping the head out of the plume.
  • RPE last: powered air-purifying or supplied-air helmets, fitted and maintained. RPE only counts toward compliance once higher-order controls are in place and it's worn correctly.

WorkSafe Queensland notes welding fumes and the UV from welding were classified as Group 1 carcinogens (carcinogenic to humans) by IARC in 2019, which is why the limit dropped.

Before 1 December: a 15-minute checklist

  • List every base metal and coating you weld (mild, galv, stainless, aluminium, hardfacing) and the consumables for each.
  • For each one, write the WES and the new WEL next to it. Flag zinc oxide and chromium VI, and in QLD note that manganese and nickel stay on the WES until 1 December 2029.
  • Find your last air monitoring report. If there isn't one, or the process has changed since, that's your "not certain on reasonable grounds" trigger.
  • Update SWMS, risk assessments, toolbox talk notes and the chemicals register entries that quote old WES numbers.
  • Check your extraction: hoods where the work actually happens, ducts clear, filters changed.
  • File monitoring results somewhere you can still find in 30 years, not in a ute glovebox.

Questions the other guides don't answer

I'm a sole trader welding on my own. Does this apply to me?

The duties sit with the person conducting a business or undertaking (PCBU), and you're a PCBU even if you're the only worker. The limits apply to any person at the workplace, including you, the client's workers nearby and anyone else in the shed.

The SDS for my wire lists different exposure values. Which one counts?

The welding code says that although SDS section 8 may list exposure values, you should always check the official list or Safe Work Australia's Hazardous Chemical Information System (HCIS) for the mandatory value. SDSs often quote overseas limits.

Does this apply on a mine site?

Mines have their own safety and health legislation, which this guide doesn't cover. Check the mine's own requirements and your regulator.

Quick answers

What is the welding fume exposure limit in Australia?

Welding fumes (not otherwise classified) have an 8-hour time-weighted average limit of 1 mg/m³. It was cut from 5 mg/m³ to 1 mg/m³ in January 2024 and stays at 1 mg/m³ in the workplace exposure limits (WEL) list that applies from 1 December 2026.

What changes on 1 December 2026?

Safe Work Australia's Workplace exposure limits for airborne contaminants (WEL list) replaces the Workplace exposure standards (WES list). Most limits are unchanged, but some that matter to welders drop, for example zinc oxide (galv fume) goes from 5 mg/m³ to 2 mg/m³ TWA, and chromium VI compounds no longer have a numeric limit because they are listed as non-threshold genotoxic carcinogens.

Does Queensland have a transition period for welding fume components?

Yes, for some. WorkSafe Queensland says most WELs apply from 1 December 2026, but 15 substances have a transition until 1 December 2029, including manganese fume, dust and compounds and nickel metal, powder and nickel sulphide roasting (fume and dust). For those, the existing WES keeps applying until then.

Do I have to do air monitoring for welding fume?

Under section 50 of Queensland's WHS Regulation 2011 (regulation 50 of the model WHS Regulations), air monitoring is required if you aren't certain on reasonable grounds whether the airborne concentration exceeds the exposure limit, or if monitoring is needed to work out whether there's a risk to health. Results must be recorded and kept for 30 years.

Can a respirator be used to meet the limit?

Safe Work Australia says protection from respiratory protective equipment can be taken into account when checking compliance with the WEL only once all reasonably practicable higher-order controls are in place and the RPE is worn correctly. Extraction and ventilation come first.

Does the 1 mg/m³ welding fume limit itself change on 1 December 2026?

No. Welding fumes (not otherwise classified) stay at an 8-hour time-weighted average of 1 mg/m³ in Safe Work Australia's Workplace exposure limits (WEL) list, the same figure that has applied since January 2024. What changes on 1 December 2026 is that the WEL list replaces the Workplace exposure standards (WES) list.

Is meeting the 1 mg/m³ welding fume limit enough after 1 December 2026?

No. The 1 mg/m³ figure is for welding fumes not otherwise classified. Individual metals and gases in the fume can have their own limits, some of which change on 1 December 2026. For example, Safe Work Australia's WEL list sets zinc oxide (dust and fume, including galv fume) at 2 mg/m³ TWA, down from the previous 5 mg/m³ fume TWA, and chromium VI compounds no longer have a numeric limit because they are listed as non-threshold genotoxic carcinogens — they must be eliminated or the risk reduced so far as reasonably practicable. In Queensland, WorkSafe Queensland says most WELs apply from 1 December 2026, but 15 substances have a transition until 1 December 2029, including manganese fume, dust and compounds and nickel metal, powder and nickel sulphide roasting (fume and dust). For those, the existing WES keeps applying until then.
