Lockout tagout (LOTO) procedure Australia: the 8 isolation steps, locks vs tags

Last reviewed 28 November 2026 · Sources linked below · Written by Jay at Andwat Co, an Australian boilermaker/fabricator (Cert III Engineering, 12+ years in the trade). About

How to lock out and tag out plant in an Australian workshop, from the Managing risks of plant Code of Practice: the 8-step isolation process, stored energy, personal locks, danger vs out-of-service tags, and QLD WHS Regulation s207, s208 and s210.

Most workshop crush and entanglement injuries don't happen while the machine is making parts. They happen while someone is clearing a jam, changing a blade, cleaning out swarf or fixing a fault, and somebody else starts it. WorkSafe Queensland's isolation film opens with exactly that: a worker cleaning a machine had his arm crushed when another worker's broom hit the foot pedal. The main switch was off, but the isolation switch on the supply had been left on. Lockout tagout (LOTO) is the fix. Here's what the Australian Code of Practice actually says, set out so you can turn it into a procedure for your own shop.

The short answer

  • Isolate before you service, clean or clear. The Managing risks of plant Code of Practice says plant should usually be isolated before maintenance or cleaning starts (model Code, section 3.6; the Queensland Code has the same wording).
  • Lock-out is the most effective isolation procedure. The Code gives an 8-step process: shut down, identify energy sources and hazards, identify isolation points, isolate, control stored energy, lock out, tag, then test (section 4.5).
  • A tag is not an isolation. "Only a lock is effective in isolating the energy source." Tags are information for other people.
  • One person, one lock, one key. Each worker should have their own lock, tag and key for each isolation point, with no duplicate keys apart from a master key kept secure for emergencies.
  • The law behind it (QLD). Work Health and Safety Regulation 2011 s210 requires operator's controls that can be locked in the "off" position to disconnect all motive power. s207 covers plant not in use, and s208(5) says removable guarding must stop the plant restarting until it's replaced, so far as is reasonably practicable. Each carries a maximum penalty of 60 penalty units (WHS Regulation 2011 (Qld)).

The 8-step lock-out process

This is the sequence from section 4.5 of the Code of Practice, with what each step looks like on a fab-shop machine.

#Code of Practice stepWhat it looks like in the shop
1Shut down the machinery and equipmentNormal stop using the operator's controls. Let moving parts come to rest. Tell the operator and anyone nearby what you're doing.
2Identify all energy sources and other hazardsMains power, hydraulics, compressed air, gas, gravity, springs, heat. Use the manual or the machine's isolation sheet, not memory. See the energy table below.
3Identify all isolation pointsMain isolator, local isolators, air ball valves, hydraulic power-pack switch, gas cylinder valves. Machines can have more than one supply.
4Isolate all energy sourcesTurn each isolation point off. The main operating switch or an e-stop is not an isolation point.
5Control or de-energise all stored energyBleed air and hydraulic pressure, lower raised tooling or beams to a rest or block them, let hot parts cool.
6Lock out all isolation pointsYour personal padlock on every isolation point, using a hasp if more than one person is working on it.
7Tag machinery controls, energy sources and other hazardsA danger tag on each lock and on the controls: your name, date, time and reason.
8Test by "trying" to reactivate the plantHit start with no one in the danger zone. Then put the controls back to off. The Code warns that a dead start button means the main power is probably isolated, not that all stored energy has gone.

Energy sources you have to find

The Code lists these energy sources: electricity (mains), battery or capacitor banks, solar panels, fuels, heat, steam, fluids or gases under pressure (water, air, steam or hydraulic oil), stored energy such as compressed springs, gravity, and radiation. In a steel shop, that usually means:

Electrical (mains)Bandsaw, drill press, guillotine, press brake, overhead craneLockable isolator switched off and locked. Unplug and control the plug for cord-connected gear.
Hydraulic pressurePress brake, ironworker, hydraulic punch, tube benderPower pack off and locked, pressure released per the manual, ram lowered or blocked
Pneumatic pressureAir clamps, air-driven feeds, compressor linesAir supply valve shut and locked, line bled down to zero
GravityRaised guillotine blade or press brake ram, a load on a crane or chain blockLowered to rest, or mechanically blocked or pinned

Heat and fuel gasOvens, preheat gear, oxy-acetylene and LPGAllowed to cool. Cylinder valves shut and lines bled (see the gas cylinder storage guide)

Locks, hasps and keys: the multi-person rules

  • Use isolation devices that actually lock: switches with built-in locks, lock-out circuit breakers, fuses and valves, plus chains, hasps (safety lock-out jaws) and safety padlocks.
  • Where possible, use an isolation device that takes more than one padlock. If more than one person is working on the plant, each worker attaches their own padlock.
  • Another way to do it is to put one lock on the isolation point and the key in a lock box, which each worker then locks separately.
  • No duplicate keys for any lock, except a master key kept in a secure location for emergencies only.
  • If more than one energy source has to be isolated, the keys to each lock-out device should be held by the same person.
  • WorkSafe Queensland's film says that when a group isolation is used, each person's name and details must be recorded on the isolation register.

Danger tags vs out of service tags

WorkSafe Queensland draws the line like this: a danger lockout tag goes on once energy sources and isolation points have been locked out while someone is working on the machine and would be at serious risk if it were operated. An out of service tag marks machinery that is faulty or damaged. Tags should identify the person who locked out the machine, the date and time, and the reason for isolation.

Taking locks off and handing the machine back

  • Locks and tags should only be removed by the person who applied them, or by the supervisor after consultation with the person who signed the tag.
  • If that person is unavailable (gone home with the key in their pocket), the lock or tag may only be removed under a management approved procedure. Write yours down before you need it.
  • All guarding must be replaced before the plant is used again. Plant with damage that poses a risk should stay out of service until those risks are controlled.
  • The Code says you should have a process for communicating and consulting with affected workers before plant is restarted after a shutdown for inspection, maintenance or cleaning.

When plant can't be fully isolated

Some jobs, such as fault-finding, teaching a robot or fine adjustments, need the machine powered. The Code says that where plant can't be isolated, you should use methods to prevent accidental operation and carry out the work under controlled procedures. QLD WHS Regulation s210(2) then requires the controls to allow operation during the work, to stop anyone except the person doing the work (or someone authorised for that purpose) from operating it, and to let it run in a way that eliminates or minimises the risk so far as is reasonably practicable.

Electrical isolation: where your job stops

Switching off and locking a lockable isolator is part of a normal LOTO. Working on fixed wiring, inside a switchboard or on the machine's electrical parts is electrical work. In Queensland, s55 of the Electrical Safety Act 2002 says a person must not perform or supervise electrical work unless they hold an electrical work licence that covers it. If the isolation itself needs a sparky, book one.

FAQ

Is lockout tagout a legal requirement in Australia?

The WHS Regulations don't use the words 'lockout tagout', but they do require the person with management or control of plant to have operator's controls that can be locked in the 'off' position to disconnect all motive power (QLD WHS Regulation s210(1)(d)) and to leave plant not in use in a safe state (s207). The Managing risks of plant Code of Practice says plant should usually be isolated before maintenance or cleaning, and calls the lock-out process the most effective isolation procedure.

Is a danger tag enough to isolate a machine?

No. The Code of Practice says a tag should not be used on its own as an isolation device; only a lock is effective in isolating the energy source. Tags are there to give information to other people at the workplace.

Who can remove a lockout lock or danger tag?

Under the Code of Practice, tags and locks should only be removed by the person who applied them, or by the supervisor after consultation with the person who signed the tag. If that person isn't available, the lock or tag may only be removed under a management approved procedure.

What is the difference between a danger tag and an out of service tag?

WorkSafe Queensland describes danger lockout tags as the ones used once energy sources and isolation points have been locked out while someone is working on the machine. Out of service tags go on machinery to show it is faulty or damaged. Tags should show who locked out the machine, the date and time, and the reason.

What if the machine has to run while it's being cleaned or adjusted?

QLD WHS Regulation s210(2) says that if operating plant during maintenance or cleaning can't be avoided, the controls must allow it to run while that work happens, must stop anyone other than the person doing the work (or someone authorised for that purpose) from operating it, and must let it run in a way that eliminates or minimises the risk so far as is reasonably practicable.
