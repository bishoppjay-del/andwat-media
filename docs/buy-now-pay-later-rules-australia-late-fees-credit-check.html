Afterpay and buy now pay later rules in Australia: late fees, credit checks and hardship

Last reviewed 17 November 2026 · Sources linked below · Written by Jay at Andwat Co, an Australian boilermaker/fabricator (Cert III Engineering, 12+ years in the trade). About

Since 10 June 2025 buy now pay later is regulated credit in Australia. What that means before Christmas: credit checks, the late-fee caps for low cost credit contracts, your hardship rights, AFCA complaints, and BNPL for work tools.

December is when the pay-in-4 plans stack up. A drill kit for the young bloke, the kids' presents, the Christmas lunch shop, all split into four. Each one looks small. Together they can eat the first two pays of January, right when a lot of us are waiting on December invoices that won't land until late in the month.

The rules changed in mid-2025, and a lot of people still think Afterpay and the like sit outside credit law. They don't any more. This page sets out what the law now says, in plain English, from ASIC, Moneysmart, Treasury and the legislation linked below. It's general information, not financial or legal advice, and it doesn't rate or recommend any provider.

The short answer

  • Since 10 June 2025, buy now pay later (BNPL) is regulated credit. The Treasury Laws Amendment (Responsible Buy Now Pay Later and Other Measures) Act 2024 brought BNPL contracts under the National Consumer Credit Protection Act 2009 (the National Credit Act) and the National Credit Code.
  • Providers need an Australian credit licence (or an application ASIC has accepted for lodgement under the transitional rules) and must be AFCA members.
  • Expect a credit check when you apply, and late or missed payments can be reported.
  • Fees are capped for plans that count as "low cost credit contracts": default fees such as late fees can't add up to more than $120 with one provider in a 12-month fee period.
  • You can ask for hardship help, and the provider generally has 21 days to respond.

What changed on 10 June 2025

ASIC's guidance says that from 10 June 2025 anyone engaging in credit activities involving buy now pay later contracts must hold an Australian credit licence with the right authorisations. Providers that had applied, and had ASIC accept the application for lodgement, could keep trading until ASIC decides the application. All of them must be members of the Australian Financial Complaints Authority.

The law also created a new category called a low cost credit contract (section 13E of the National Credit Code). A typical pay-in-4 plan with no interest and limited fees is the kind of contract it was built for. Providers of low cost credit contracts can choose to follow modified responsible lending obligations, a lighter version of the usual checks. ASIC's Regulatory Guide 281 explains them. Under either version the provider still has to make reasonable inquiries about your requirements and financial situation, take reasonable steps to verify it, and assess whether the contract is unsuitable for you. A BNPL contract that doesn't meet the low cost definition is regulated like other credit.

Late fees: the caps in the regulations

To count as a low cost credit contract, the fees on a plan, added to any other low cost credit contracts you have with the same provider, must not be able to go over these caps in a "fee period" (regulation 69G of the National Consumer Credit Protection Regulations 2010, as inserted in 2025):

Kind of feeFirst 12-month fee periodEach later 12-month fee period
Default fees (for example late payment fees)$120$120
Other fees (for example account or establishment fees)$200$125
Most you can be charged in total$320$245

Treasury's explanatory statement makes a few points worth knowing:

  • The cap is per provider, across all your plans with that provider, not per purchase. If you use two providers, each one has its own cap.
  • The first fee period starts from when you first had a low cost credit contract with that provider after the rules began in June 2025. After that, fee periods run back to back. Closing your account and opening a new one with the same provider doesn't reset you to the first period.
  • Government charges and enforcement expenses don't count towards the cap.

The caps are a ceiling, not a price list. Your provider's own late fee may be lower, and its terms say what it actually charges. Moneysmart also warns about costs outside the BNPL fees: your bank's overdrawn or dishonour fees if a payment bounces, and interest if you pay the instalments with a credit card.

Credit checks and your credit report

Moneysmart says every BNPL application triggers a credit check, and a lot of credit checks can be a red flag for lenders. BNPL arrangements, late fees and applications may appear on your credit report, and late or missed payments can be reported and reduce your credit score. If you're planning to apply for a car, ute or home loan in the new year, that's a reason to keep December's plans few and paid on time.

If you can't make a payment

  • Ask for hardship help early. Under section 72 of the National Credit Code you can tell the provider, verbally or in writing, that you can't meet your repayments (a hardship notice). ASIC says the provider has 21 days to give its decision if it has enough information, or 21 days to ask you for more. If it asks and you don't reply, it has 28 days from the request to respond.
  • Complain to the provider first. Moneysmart says all BNPL providers are required to have complaints and hardship services.
  • Then AFCA. If the provider doesn't fix it, you can take the complaint to the Australian Financial Complaints Authority for free. AFCA says it handles BNPL complaints about responsible lending, hardship, debt collection and credit reporting.
  • Free help. The National Debt Helpline (1800 007 007) puts you in touch with a free financial counsellor.

Using pay-in-4 for work tools

The National Credit Code is about credit for personal, domestic or household purposes. If you're a sole trader buying a welder or a set of batteries mainly for the business, the consumer credit protections above may not apply the same way, and the provider's own business terms matter more. Read the terms before you check out, and keep the receipt for your tax records whichever way you pay. For the tax side of buying tools, see the record-keeping guide and the Christmas cash flow guide.

A simple plan before you tap "pay in 4"

  • Write every plan down in one place: what it was for, the provider, and all four payment dates.
  • Line the dates up against your paydays. Fortnightly pays and pay-in-4 dates rarely line up neatly, so a payment can come out the day before your pay goes in.
  • Add up what's still owing after 25 December. That's the January bill nobody budgets for, and it's due while many trade invoices are still unpaid over the shutdown.
  • Keep enough in the account each payment day to avoid a bank dishonour fee as well as the late fee.
  • If you're getting near the edge, stop adding new plans and ring the provider before you miss a payment, not after.

Questions people ask

Is buy now pay later regulated in Australia?

Yes. Since 10 June 2025, buy now pay later contracts are regulated credit under the National Consumer Credit Protection Act 2009 and the National Credit Code. Providers need an Australian credit licence, must be members of the Australian Financial Complaints Authority (AFCA), must follow responsible lending obligations and must consider hardship requests.

Is there a cap on Afterpay-style late fees?

For a buy now pay later plan to count as a low cost credit contract, regulation 69G of the National Credit Regulations caps the fees one provider can charge you across all those contracts. Default fees, such as late fees, are capped at $120 per 12-month fee period, and other fees at $200 in the first 12 months and $125 in each later 12 months. Check your own provider's terms for its actual fees.

Does buy now pay later affect my credit score?

It can. Moneysmart says applying for buy now pay later triggers a credit check, and applications, arrangements and late or missed payments can show on your credit report, which lenders look at when you apply for a car loan or home loan.

What can I do if I can't make a BNPL payment?

Contact the provider and ask for financial hardship assistance. Under section 72 of the National Credit Code the provider generally has 21 days to respond, or to ask you for more information. If you can't sort out a complaint with the provider, you can take it to AFCA for free. The National Debt Helpline is 1800 007 007.
