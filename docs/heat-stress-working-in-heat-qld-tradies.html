Working in heat in QLD: heat stress, water, rest and what the Code actually says

Last reviewed 23 November 2026 · Sources linked below · Written by Jay at Andwat Co, an Australian boilermaker/fabricator (Cert III Engineering, 12+ years in the trade). About

What WorkSafe Queensland and the Managing the work environment and facilities Code of Practice 2021 say about working in heat: there is no workplace exposure standard for heat stress, drinking water must be free, rest, acclimatisation, first aid (s42) and emergency plans for heat stroke (s43).

Queensland summers are hard on outdoor crews and hot workshops. This guide only restates what WorkSafe Queensland and the current Managing the work environment and facilities Code of Practice 2021 (varied 1 September 2026) say. It is not a medical guideline and it does not set a "safe" air temperature for your site.

The short answer

  • The duty (Code of Practice, Heat and cold): workers carrying out work in extreme heat must be able to carry out that work without a risk to their physical and psychosocial health and safety, so far as is reasonably practicable. Conditions and workers' health must be monitored when work involves prolonged or repeated exposure to heat.
  • There is no workplace exposure standard or limit for heat stress in Queensland. WorkSafe Queensland says you should not set a safe or unsafe limit from air temperature alone.
  • Heatstroke is a medical emergency. WorkSafe Queensland describes heatstroke as a core body temperature above 40.5°C. The Code lists early signs of heat-related illness that need immediate assistance: dizziness, fatigue, headache, nausea, breathlessness, clammy skin or difficulty remaining alert.
  • Drinking water (Code 3.2): clean drinking water must be provided free of charge for workers at all times, easy to get to, close to hot or strenuous work, and separate from toilets or washing facilities. The Code says the water should be at or below 24°C, and supplied so workers do not drink from a shared container.
  • First aid and emergencies: WorkSafe Queensland says first aid arrangements under WHS Regulation section 42 must be suitable for the environment, including heat, and emergency plans under section 43 must cover heat stroke.

There is no "safe temperature" number you can post on the wall

WorkSafe Queensland is explicit: there is no workplace exposure standard or limit for heat stress, and air temperature on its own is not enough to decide what is safe or unsafe. A normal core body temperature is about 37°C. Feeling uncomfortable is not the same as being harmed, if the body can still cope.

The Code makes the same distinction between a condition that threatens health and safety and a feeling of discomfort. The risk increases as conditions move further away from what is generally accepted as comfortable. Heat-related illness can come from high air temperatures, high thermal radiation or high humidity (the Code gives foundries, commercial kitchens and laundries as examples).

Comfort figures in the official sources are about comfort, not a legal exposure limit:

  • WorkSafe Queensland notes that people often find about 20–27°C and 35–60% relative humidity comfortable.
  • The Code says optimum comfort for sedentary work is between 20 and 26°C, depending on the time of year and clothing, and that workers doing physical work usually prefer a lower temperature range.

WorkSafe Queensland says heat stress depends on three groups of factors: the environment, the work process, and the individual (including how acclimatised they are). The Code adds personal factors such as some prescription medication, age, health, the level of physical activity, pregnancy and breastfeeding, clothing, and how long someone is exposed; and environmental factors such as air temperature, humidity, air movement and radiant heat.

Controls the Code and WorkSafe actually list

If exposure to extreme heat cannot be eliminated, the Code says the risk of heat-related illness must be minimised so far as is reasonably practicable. Examples it lists include:

  • increase air movement with fans
  • install air-conditioners or evaporative coolers to lower air temperature
  • isolate workers from indoor heat sources, for example by insulating plant, pipes and walls
  • remove heated air or steam from hot processes with local exhaust ventilation
  • use mechanical aids to help with manual tasks
  • alter work schedules so work is done at cooler times
  • slow down the pace of work if possible
  • provide a supply of cool drinking water
  • provide a cool, well-ventilated area where workers can take rest breaks, and give workers more job control (for example, a chance to choose when they take breaks)
  • give workers who are not used to hot conditions a chance to acclimatise, for example through job rotation and regular rest breaks
  • ensure light clothing is worn so air can move and sweat can evaporate
  • train workers about the hazards of working in heat and how to recognise and act on symptoms of heat-related illness

The Code also says that, where work involves prolonged or repeated exposure to heat, information, training and instruction should include how to recognise early symptoms of heat-related illness in themselves and others, how to follow safe work procedures, and to report problems immediately.

WorkSafe Queensland's heat stress page adds practical controls in the same direction: modify the environment (shade, insulation, ventilation, cooling) and modify the work (mechanical aids, cooler start times, rest areas, work-rest with cool water, frequent small drinks, removing PPE during breaks, electrolytes when sweat loss is high, a buddy system, and training).

This guide does not add a work-rest timetable or an air-temperature cutoff. Those are not in the two official sources used here.

Outdoor work, shade and UV

The Code (Outdoor work) says outdoor workers should have access to shelter for eating meals and taking breaks, and to protect them in adverse weather — for example sheds, caravans, tents, windbreaks or portable shade canopies. In some situations, vehicles or public facilities may provide short-term shelter.

Where a risk cannot be eliminated, the Code says you still have a duty to minimise it, including suitable PPE. Protection against solar ultraviolet radiation should be provided, for example by:

  • reorganising outdoor work if possible so workers do alternative tasks, or work in shade, when the sun is most intense (the Code states 10.00am–2.00pm, and 11.00am–3.00pm during daylight saving time)
  • providing personal protective clothing like a wide-brim hat, a long-sleeved collared shirt, long pants, sunglasses and sunscreen

WorkSafe Queensland also says long work clothing is not a cause of heat illness, and that outdoor workers should still wear long pants and collared long-sleeve shirts because they can be exposed to five to ten times more UV radiation than indoor workers.

Drinking water in detail

Section 3.2 of the Code is specific:

  • clean drinking water must be provided free of charge at all times
  • it should be positioned where workers can easily access it, and close to where hot or strenuous work is being done, to reduce the likelihood of dehydration or heat stress
  • the supply should be separate from toilet or washing facilities, to avoid contamination
  • the temperature of the drinking water should be at or below 24°C (for example by refrigerating it, providing non-contaminated ice, or shading pipes and storage from the sun)
  • water should be supplied hygienically, so workers do not drink directly from a shared container (for example a drinking fountain with an upward jet, or disposable or washable containers)
  • water used for some industrial processes or fire protection may not be suitable to drink and should be marked with signs warning that it is unfit for drinking

The Code also notes that the WHS Regulation sets out duties, including for principal contractors, to provide amenities such as drinking water for construction work.

First aid, heat stroke and what to do when someone is unwell

The Code says first aid is vital when a worker is suffering from a heat-related illness, and that immediate assistance should be provided if a worker has any of: dizziness, fatigue, headache, nausea, breathlessness, clammy skin or difficulty remaining alert.

WorkSafe Queensland describes heatstroke as a core body temperature above 40.5°C and a medical emergency. It also says first aid arrangements under section 42 of the WHS Regulation must be suitable for the environment, including heat, and that emergency plans under section 43 must cover heat stroke.

This page does not set out a first-aid treatment protocol. Follow your trained first aiders, your emergency plan, and medical advice. See also the site guide on first aid kits, first aider numbers and training in Queensland.

FAQ

Is there a legal maximum air temperature for work in Queensland?

No workplace exposure standard or limit is set for heat stress. WorkSafe Queensland says you should not decide what is safe or unsafe from air temperature alone. The Code requires the risk to be managed so far as is reasonably practicable, including monitoring, training, rest, acclimatisation and cool drinking water.

What drinking water does the Code require?

Clean drinking water must be provided free of charge at all times. It should be easy to get to, close to hot or strenuous work, separate from toilets or washing facilities, at or below 24°C, and supplied so workers do not drink from a shared container.

What is heatstroke according to WorkSafe Queensland?

WorkSafe Queensland describes heatstroke as a core body temperature above 40.5°C and says it is a medical emergency. Emergency plans under WHS Regulation section 43 must cover heat stroke.

Should outdoor tradies switch to short sleeves in the heat?

WorkSafe Queensland says long work clothing is not a cause of heat illness, and outdoor workers should still wear long pants and a collared long-sleeve shirt because they can be exposed to five to ten times more UV radiation than indoor workers. The Code also lists a wide-brim hat, long-sleeved collared shirt, long pants, sunglasses and sunscreen as examples of sun protection.

Do first aid rules change when the site is hot?

WorkSafe Queensland says first aid arrangements under WHS Regulation section 42 must be suitable for the environment, including heat. The general first aid duties in section 42 still apply.
