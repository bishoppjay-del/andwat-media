Workplace emergency plan requirements in QLD: WHS Regulation s43 explained (with checklist)

Last reviewed 26 November 2026 · Sources linked below · Written by Jay at Andwat Co, an Australian boilermaker/fabricator (Cert III Engineering, 12+ years in the trade). About

What a Queensland workplace emergency plan must cover under WHS Regulation s43: evacuation, calling 000, first aid, communication, testing and training. Plus drills, shared sites, confined space and fall arrest rescue, and a one-page checklist.

Most small shops have an emergency plan in someone's head: "get out the roller door and meet at the ute." That isn't a plan under Queensland law. Section 43 of the Work Health and Safety Regulation 2011 (Qld) needs it to cover specific things, be tested, and be taught to your workers. It doesn't need to be long. Here's what the Regulation, WorkSafe QLD's Code of Practice and Safe Work Australia's guidance actually say, in plain English, for fab shops, crews and small trade businesses.

The short answer

  • Prepare it (s43(1)): a PCBU at a workplace must make sure an emergency plan is prepared for the workplace. Safe Work Australia's Emergency plans fact sheet describes it as a written set of instructions, and says it includes workers who may work at multiple workplaces.
  • Keep it working (s43(2)): you must maintain the plan so it stays effective.
  • Fit it to your workplace (s43(3)): you must have regard to the nature of the work, the hazards, the size and location of the workplace, and the number and composition of workers and other people there.
  • Use it (s43(4)): you must implement the plan in an emergency.
  • Penalty: s43(1), (2) and (4) each carry a maximum penalty of 60 penalty units. A Queensland penalty unit has been A$172.70 since 1 July 2026 (Penalties and Sentences Regulation 2025).

What the plan must cover

Section 43(1) lists seven things. The left column is the law. The right column is how I'd apply it in a small steel shop or on a small site.

What s43 requiresWhat that looks like for a small fab shop or site
An effective response to an emergency (s43(1)(a)(i))Who does what for each likely emergency at your site: fire (hot work, grinding sparks), gas cylinder leak, serious injury, chemical spill, storm. Name the person who coordinates.
Evacuation procedures (s43(1)(a)(ii))Exits, routes and the assembly point, how you account for everyone (including subbies and visitors), and help for anyone who can't get out on their own.
Notifying emergency services at the earliest opportunity (s43(1)(a)(iii))Who rings 000, the site address and access point to give them, and who meets the ambulance or fire crew at the gate.
Medical treatment and assistance (s43(1)(a)(iv))Where the first aid kit is, who the first aiders are, and the nearest hospital. Ties in with your first aid duty under s42.
Effective communication (s43(1)(a)(v))How the coordinator tells everyone: alarm, air horn, radio channel, phone tree. Think about a noisy shop where people are wearing earmuffs.
Testing, including how often (s43(1)(b))Write down how and how often you test it (drills, alarm tests), and when the next one is due.
Information, training and instruction (s43(1)(c))How workers learn the procedures: induction for new starters and subbies, refreshers, and specific training for wardens and first aiders.

Safe Work Australia lists the kinds of emergencies to plan for as fire, explosion, medical emergency, rescues, incidents with hazardous chemicals, bomb threats, armed confrontations and natural disasters. It also says to think about external hazards, such as a chemical storage facility across the road.

How detailed does it have to be?

Not very, for a small business. Safe Work Australia says emergency plans "do not necessarily have to be lengthy or complex". They should be easy to understand and tailored to the workplace. Its fact sheet suggests practical inclusions such as:

  • contact details for people with roles in the plan (wardens, first aid officers)
  • local emergency services contacts (police, fire brigade, poison information centre)
  • how people are alerted (siren, bell alarm)
  • evacuation procedures, including help for anyone with hearing, vision or mobility impairment
  • a site map showing fire protection equipment, emergency exits and assembly points
  • how you'd warn neighbouring businesses
  • post-incident follow-up, such as notifying the regulator and arranging medical treatment or counselling

It also says the plan, or a summary of its key parts, should be easy for workers to get to or on display, for example on a notice board. The Managing the work environment and facilities Code of Practice 2021 says evacuation procedures should be displayed somewhere prominent and workers must be instructed and trained in them.

Safe Work Australia also publishes a free emergency plan template (DOCX) you can fill in.

Lone workers and mobile tradies

If you work alone or move between sites, you still need a plan. Safe Work Australia says special consideration may be needed for workers who travel, work alone or work in remote locations, and its checklist asks whether workers working alone know what to do in an emergency. For a mobile welder that usually means: a charged phone with reception (or another way to call for help), someone who knows where you are and when you should check in, the site address ready for 000, and a fire extinguisher and first aid kit in the ute.

Shared sites and construction projects

On a shared workplace, every PCBU has to consult, cooperate and coordinate with the other duty holders, so far as is reasonably practicable. Safe Work Australia says a master emergency plan could be prepared that all duty holders use, and gives construction sites as an example. The Queensland Code says the plan for a shared workplace (for example a construction site) should be coordinated by the person with management or control of the workplace, which may be the principal contractor, in consultation with the businesses there.

In practice: if you're a subbie on a builder's site, get the site emergency procedures at induction, know the assembly point, and make sure your own crew's high-risk tasks (hot work, confined space, work at height) have their own rescue arrangements that fit into the site plan. The Code also says workers and their health and safety representatives must be consulted when the plan is reviewed, and that local emergency services should be consulted when developing the plan if the consequences of an emergency could be significant.

Testing and drills

The Regulation makes you test the emergency procedures and write the testing frequency into the plan, but it doesn't set the frequency. The Queensland Code lists regular evacuation practice drills at least every 12 months among the things emergency procedures should address where relevant, and says procedures must be tested in line with the plan they're in. Safe Work Australia's checklist asks whether someone is responsible for documenting and keeping the results of practice runs. A dated drill record (who was there, how long it took, what went wrong, what you changed) is your evidence that the plan has been tested.

When to review the plan

Safe Work Australia gives these examples of when to review and, if needed, revise the plan:

  • changes to the workplace, such as relocating or refurbishing
  • changes in the number or make-up of staff, including more temporary contractors
  • when new activities are introduced
  • after the plan has been tested

Extra emergency rules for higher-risk work

Some work has its own emergency or rescue duties in the Queensland WHS Regulation on top of s43:

  • Confined spaces (s74): establish first aid and rescue procedures for an emergency in a confined space, practise them as needed so they're efficient and effective, and start rescue from outside the space as soon as practicable. See the confined space entry permit guide.
  • Fall arrest systems (s80): if you use fall arrest to control the risk, you must set up emergency procedures including rescue, test them so they're effective, and train relevant workers in them. A harness without a rescue plan doesn't meet this. See the working at heights guide.
  • Hazardous chemicals over manifest quantity (s361): if a Schedule 11 hazardous chemical at the workplace exceeds its manifest quantity, you must give a copy of your emergency plan to the primary emergency service organisation and revise it if they recommend changes in writing. WorkSafe QLD has more on emergency planning for hazardous chemicals.

Safe Work Australia's fact sheet also flags confined spaces, fall arrest harness use, asbestos, hazardous chemicals, and demolition and refurbishment sites as higher-risk workplaces that may need more detail in their plans.

Your building's fire and evacuation plan is separate

WorkSafe QLD says your WHS emergency plan is in addition to the fire and evacuation plan required under the Building Fire Safety Regulation 2008, and the two can be combined into one plan for your workplace. If you lease a shed or unit, check what your landlord or building manager already has. The Queensland Fire Department publishes fire and evacuation plan templates and evacuation practice records.

One-page emergency plan checklist (small fab shop or site)

  1. Site address, nearest cross street and the gate emergency services should use.
  2. Emergency coordinator (and a backup) named, with mobile numbers.
  3. Emergency numbers: 000, Poisons Information Centre (13 11 26), nearest hospital, power and gas supplier faults lines, the site owner or builder.
  4. Likely emergencies listed for this site: fire, gas leak, serious injury, chemical spill, storm, plus any confined space or fall arrest rescue.
  5. Response steps for each, in a few lines, including shutting down plant, isolating gas and power where safe.
  6. Alarm or warning method that works over shop noise and earmuffs.
  7. Evacuation routes, assembly point, and how you count heads (sign-in sheet, visitor book).
  8. First aiders, first aid kit and extinguisher locations on a simple site map.
  9. Help for anyone who can't evacuate on their own.
  10. Testing: drill frequency (at least every 12 months is the Code's benchmark) and the date of the next one.
  11. Training: covered at induction for new workers and subbies, refreshed, plus specific training for anyone with a role.
  12. After an incident: notify WorkSafe QLD if it's notifiable, preserve the site, record it, and review the plan.
  13. Plan displayed (or a summary) where workers can see it, and a review date set.

FAQ

Is an emergency plan a legal requirement in Queensland?

Yes. Section 43 of the Work Health and Safety Regulation 2011 (Qld) says a person conducting a business or undertaking (PCBU) at a workplace must make sure an emergency plan is prepared for the workplace, keep it effective, and put it into action in an emergency. Each of those duties carries a maximum penalty of 60 penalty units.

What must a workplace emergency plan include?

Under s43(1): emergency procedures covering an effective response, evacuation, notifying emergency services at the earliest opportunity, medical treatment and assistance, and communication between the person coordinating the response and everyone at the workplace. It must also cover testing of those procedures (including how often) and information, training and instruction for relevant workers.

How often should we do an evacuation drill?

The Regulation leaves the testing frequency to you, but the plan must state it. Queensland's Managing the work environment and facilities Code of Practice 2021 lists regular evacuation practice drills at least every 12 months among the things emergency procedures should address where relevant. Safe Work Australia also suggests documenting and keeping the results of practice runs.

Who prepares the emergency plan on a shared construction site?

Every PCBU still has the duty, but Safe Work Australia says shared workplaces such as construction sites can use a master emergency plan that all duty holders use. The Queensland Code says the plan for a shared workplace should be coordinated by the person with management or control of the workplace, which may be the principal contractor, in consultation with the businesses there.
