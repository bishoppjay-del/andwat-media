Galvanizing vent and drain holes: sizes and placement for RHS, SHS and CHS (AU)

Last reviewed 24 November 2026 · Sources linked below · Written by Jay at Andwat Co, an Australian boilermaker/fabricator (Cert III Engineering, 12+ years in the trade). About

How big should vent and drain holes be for hot dip galvanizing? GAA minimum hole sizes for SHS, RHS and CHS, where to put them, internal venting, overlaps, snipes and a shop drawing check.

Try the free tool: the galvanizing vent and drain hole lookup uses the GAA 2026 chart on this page. Pick a section and see the minimum hole sizes.

The galvanizer rings: your handrail's been knocked back. The posts are capped, there's one 6 mm hole in the middle of each cap, and they won't dip it. Or worse, it went in, came out with bare patches inside and zinc lumps on every cap. Vent and drain holes are a two-minute job on the drawing and an expensive one after the steel's welded up. Here's what the Galvanizers Association of Australia (GAA) says about sizing and placing them, with its minimum hole table for common RHS, SHS and CHS.

Why the holes matter (it's a safety issue, not just a finish issue)

Hot dip galvanizing dips the whole fabrication in cleaning and pre-treatment solutions, then in molten zinc at about 450°C. Anything enclosed has to let air out and liquid and zinc in and out again. GAA's Best Practice Design for Galvanizing chart sums it up:

  • Sealed unit: superheated steam inside leads to explosions. GAA states completely sealed fabrications cannot be galvanized.
  • One hole: it will vent steam, but the unit floats.
  • Two holes (one each end): the unit can vent and drain, and gets galvanized inside and out.

Undersized or badly placed holes also cause trapped pre-treatment fluid, "blowouts" (fluid forced out under pressure, leaving bare spots), uncoated insides, zinc pooling and lumpy drips, per GAA's Design Manual.

The basic rules (GAA)

  • Minimum Ø10 mm, and at least the same as the steel thickness. Smaller only if your galvanizer agrees. Bigger is always better.
  • Open ends are best. If you can leave hollow section ends open, do it: it gives the best finish.
  • Capped ends need holes at both ends: at least one vent hole at each end, off centre, diagonally opposite and as near the ends as practical.
  • Size by the 25% rule: pipe/CHS, RHS and SHS need vent and drain holes each equal to at least 25% of the section's diagonal, or several holes with the same total area.
  • Place at the high and low points as the piece hangs in the bath, close to corners and connections, and in the same plane as the fabrication. Think about where the galvanizer will hang it (GAA suggests thinking 45 degrees).
  • Not only in the centre of end plates or connections. GAA rates a centre hole "unsatisfactory": it traps fluid and zinc.
  • Big hollow vessels: a vent and drain hole of at least 50 mm diameter for every 0.5 m³ of enclosed volume.

Worked example: 50×50 SHS

Diagonal of a 50×50 section ≈ √(50² + 50²) ≈ 70.7 mm. 25% of that ≈ 17.7 mm, so one 18 mm hole at each end.
Two holes with the same total area: 18 ÷ √2 ≈ 12.7, so two 13 mm holes.
Four holes: 18 ÷ 2 = 9 mm, which is under the 10 mm minimum, so four 10 mm holes.
Those match the GAA chart below. Round up to the next drill or plasma size you actually have, never down.

GAA's online Design Manual tables round a few sizes a little smaller than the chart (for example 35 mm vs 40 mm for one hole in 100×100 SHS). Where the two differ, use the bigger hole or ask your galvanizer. GAA notes that longer lengths and bigger volumes may need the hollow vessel rule instead. The chart also gives crop (corner cut) options in place of round holes; check it if you'd rather crop the corners of end caps.

Connected hollow sections: external, notch and internal venting

  • External venting (GAA's preferred approach): put vent/drain holes on the outside, as close as possible to the joint between members, with drain holes at the lowest point matched by vent holes at the opposite end and face. Keep them near corners and off faces where draining zinc will run over other surfaces.
  • Notch venting: notches or cut-outs, quick with a laser or plasma, drain well and avoid zinc traps at CHS intersections. GAA warns sharp notches raise stress concentrations; agree notch sizes with your galvanizer.
  • Internal venting: best practice is cutting out the whole internal section of the joint. If you use holes, their total area must equal a circle 50% of the joint's diagonal. Add an external inspection hole of at least 10 mm at each internally vented joint so the galvanizer can confirm it, and mark the drawings "Internally Vented". Some galvanizers avoid internal venting because they can't always verify it.
  • Handrails: if a rail runs continuously through a stanchion, the rail portion inside the stanchion needs internal venting. Single-plane modules bolted together on site galvanize better than multi-plane welded runs.

Beams, channels, angles and end plates: snipes

End plates, gussets, cleats and stiffeners stop zinc draining off hot rolled sections. Crop their corners ("snipes") or add holes in the corners. GAA's snipe guide gives nominal snipe sizes of 25×25 mm or 30×30 mm depending on the section and its size; for small sections a hole is often the better option. Check the guide on the GAA chart for your section.

Overlapping plates and back-to-back members

Seal-welded overlaps can trap condensation or let pre-treatment chemicals in through weld pinholes. GAA's 2026 chart recommends, by area of overlap:

Area of overlapRecommended action (GAA 2026 chart)
Up to 10,000 mm²Fully sealed weld
10,000–100,000 mm²2 × Ø12 mm or larger holes at the corners (diagonally opposite, in one member only), or 2 × 25 mm or larger gaps in the weld at the corners
100,000–250,000 mm²4 × Ø12 mm or larger holes at the corners, or 4 × 25 mm or larger weld gaps at the corners
250,000 mm² and overHoles of 12 mm or more (or 25 mm weld gaps) at the corners and around the edge at least every 300 mm

GAA's older Venting & Draining Guide web page uses different cut-offs (for example a 10 mm hole for 10,000–40,000 mm²), so confirm with your galvanizer. Very large overlaps are best designed out.

Where AS/NZS 4680 fits

AS/NZS 4680:2025, Hot dip galvanized coatings on fabricated iron and steel articles – Specifications and test methods, replaced AS/NZS 4680:2006 in June 2025. It covers the coating: properties, thickness, appearance and test methods. Its preface says the new title makes it clear that design requirements are covered separately, and that it's read with AS/NZS 2312.2 (hot dip galvanizing for corrosion protection of structural steel). So "galv to AS/NZS 4680" on a drawing sets the coating requirement; it doesn't size your holes. That's the GAA design guidance and your galvanizer's acceptance rules.

Shop drawing check before you cut

  1. Every hollow section is either open-ended or has vent and drain holes at both ends.
  2. Hole sizes meet the GAA table for the section (Ø10 mm minimum and at least the steel thickness), rounded up to tooling you have.
  3. Holes are off centre, diagonally opposite, near corners and at the high/low points for how the piece will hang.
  4. Connected members are vented externally, or internally vented joints are marked "Internally Vented" with a Ø10 mm inspection hole.
  5. End plates, gussets, cleats and stiffeners are sniped or holed in the corners.
  6. Seal-welded overlaps over 10,000 mm² have vent holes or weld gaps.
  7. The assembly fits your galvanizer's bath, or is split into modules to avoid double dipping.
  8. Temporary ID uses tags or water-based markers, not oil-based paint pens or stickers (GAA). Permanent ID is embossed, punched or welded.
  9. Vent and drain holes and the coating spec are an ITP hold or check point before dispatch to the galvanizer.

Thin, unbraced plate and long asymmetric frames can distort in the bath: GAA notes steel grows around 5 mm per metre at 450°C. Ask your galvanizer early on anything long, thin or mixed-thickness.

FAQ

What size vent hole do I need for hot dip galvanizing?

The Galvanizers Association of Australia (GAA) says no vent hole should be under 10 mm, and holes should be at least as big as the steel thickness. For hollow sections, each end needs vent and drain area equal to one hole about 25% of the section's diagonal. For 50x50 SHS, GAA's chart gives one 18 mm hole, two 13 mm holes or four 10 mm holes at each end. Bigger is better.

Where should vent and drain holes go on RHS or SHS?

As close as possible to the high and low points of the section as it hangs in the bath, diagonally opposite each other, close to corners and connections, and in the same plane as the fabrication. GAA says holes must not be placed only in the centre of end plates, because they trap pre-treatment fluid and zinc.

Can you galvanize a fully sealed hollow section?

No. GAA's design chart says completely sealed fabrications cannot be galvanized: liquid trapped inside turns to superheated steam in the molten zinc and can cause an explosion. With one hole the section can vent but floats. You need holes at both ends so it can vent and drain.

Does AS/NZS 4680 tell me how big to make vent holes?

AS/NZS 4680:2025 is the coating standard: it covers specifications and test methods for hot dip galvanized coatings. Its preface says design requirements are covered separately, and it's read with AS/NZS 2312.2. For practical hole sizes and placement, fabricators use the GAA design guidance and confirm with their galvanizer.
