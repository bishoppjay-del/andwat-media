Workplace noise in QLD: the 85 dB(A) standard, hearing protection and audiometric testing

The noise exposure standard is 85 dB(A) over 8 hours or a 140 dB(C) peak. How the 3 dB rule cuts safe time, the ready reckoner, hearing protector classes, and Queensland's audiometric testing rule (since 29 July 2025: within 3 months, then every 2 years).

Grinding, gouging, hammering plate, the press brake banging away and a compressor running in the corner: a fab shop is one of the noisiest places a tradie works. Noise-induced hearing loss is permanent, and in Queensland the rules got tighter in 2025 when audiometric testing became mandatory again. Here's what the law says, how to work out whether you're over the limit, and what to do about it.

The short answer

  • Exposure standard: LAeq,8h of 85 dB(A) (the average over an 8-hour shift) or LC,peak of 140 dB(C) (any single peak). Exceed either and you're over.
  • 3 dB rule: every 3 dB louder halves the safe time. 85 dB(A) for 8 hours is the same dose as 91 dB(A) for 2 hours or 100 dB(A) for 15 minutes.
  • The 1 metre test: if you have to raise your voice to talk to someone about a metre away, the noise is probably too high.
  • Queensland audiometric testing (since 29 July 2025): if you require a worker to wear hearing protection because their exposure exceeds the standard, you must provide a hearing test within 3 months of them starting that work and then at least every 2 years.
  • Hearing protection is the last line, not the first. Eliminate, substitute, isolate and engineer the noise down first.

What the law actually says (Queensland)

  • Section 56, WHS Regulation 2011 (Qld): defines the exposure standard for noise as LAeq,8h 85 dB(A) or LC,peak 140 dB(C), both determined in line with AS/NZS 1269.1:2005.
  • Section 57: a PCBU must manage the risk of hearing loss from noise under the general risk management rules (Part 3.1), and must ensure the noise a worker is exposed to does not exceed the exposure standard. Maximum penalty: 60 penalty units.
  • Section 58 (audiometric testing): applies to a worker the PCBU requires to use PPE to protect against hearing loss from noise above the exposure standard. The PCBU must provide audiometric testing within 3 months of the worker starting the work and, in any event, at least every 2 years. Maximum penalty: 60 penalty units. Audiometric testing means pure tone air conduction threshold tests of each ear.
  • Section 797 (transition): section 58 was reinserted by the Work Health and Safety and Other Legislation Amendment Regulation 2024 (commenced 29 July 2024) with a one-year delay, so the testing duty started on 29 July 2025. The 3-month rule doesn't apply to workers who were already doing the work before that date, and the 2-year periods start on or after 29 July 2025.
  • Section 59: designers, manufacturers, importers and suppliers of plant must keep noise emission as low as reasonably practicable and pass on noise emission information. Ask your supplier for it before you buy or hire.

WorkSafe Queensland's Managing noise and preventing hearing loss at work Code of Practice 2021 (updated September 2026 for the audiometric testing rules) is the approved code in QLD. Safe Work Australia's model Code of Practice was also updated on 6 November 2026.

How much noise is too much? The 3 dB rule

Decibels are logarithmic: +3 dB is double the sound energy, so it does the same damage in half the time. From Table 1 of the Code of Practice (no hearing protection):

For reference, the Code's table of typical sound levels lists a sheet-metal workshop at 100 dB(A), a rivet hammer at 130 dB(A) and a lathe at 80 dB(A). At 100 dB(A) the whole day's allowance is gone in about 15 minutes. Your actual levels depend on your tools, discs, job and building, so measure rather than guess.

Working out a shift's exposure: the ready reckoner

Appendix D of the Code has a points table. Look up each noisy task's level and duration, add the points, and compare with 100 points = the 85 dB(A) standard.

  • The Code's own example: a task at 93 dB(A) for 1 hour gives 80 points, and a task at 120 dB(A) for 1 minute gives 670 points. Total 750 points, which converts to an LAeq,8h of about 94 dB(A), well over the standard. The one-minute task is the one to fix first.
  • Reading Table 9 for a typical fab day: if a measurement showed grinding at 97 dB(A) for 2 hours, that alone is 400 points, four times the standard, before you add anything else.
  • Long shifts: for shifts of 10 to under 14 hours add +1 dB to the LAeq,8h; 14 to under 20 hours add +2; 20 hours or more add +3. For more than five days a week, use the weekly averaging method in AS/NZS 1269.1:2005.

Who does the noise assessment, and how

  • A competent person, following AS/NZS 1269.1:2005. That can be an occupational hygienist, or you or a worker if you have the skills and experience.
  • Measure during a typical shift, covering each task's noise level and how long people are exposed to it.
  • Take readings 10 to 20 cm from the worker's ear. A hand-held integrating sound level meter usually gives the most useful information; noise dosimeters suit workers who move around a lot.
  • Include background noise (extraction fans, compressors) and measure each source separately so you know what to fix first.
  • One assessment can cover a group of workers with the same exposure.
  • Repeat it when machinery is added or removed, workload or equipment changes, the building changes, or people spend different amounts of time in noisy areas.

Controls that actually work in a fab shop

Work down the hierarchy. Examples from the Code that suit steel work:

  • Change the method: bending in a vice or press is quieter than hammering, welding is generally quieter than riveting, and lowering material in a controlled way is quieter than dropping it on the floor.
  • Buy quiet: ask suppliers for noise emission data (section 59 requires manufacturers to provide it and suppliers to take reasonable steps to pass it on) and put noise in your purchasing or hire spec.
  • Isolate and distance: outdoors, sound drops by about 6 dB each time you double the distance from a small source. Indoors it drops less, so screens, enclosures and keeping people out of the noisy bay matter.
  • Engineering: silencers on compressed-air exhausts and blow guns, damping (rubber) or stiffening on rattling panels, cushioning where steel hits steel, lower drop heights, and sound-absorbing linings on hard surfaces.
  • Maintenance: worn bearings and gears, poor lubrication, blunt blades, loose parts, unbalanced rotating parts and air leaks all add noise.
  • Admin: do noisy work when fewer people are around, warn people before it starts, keep non-essential people out, provide quiet break areas, and rotate jobs before anyone reaches the limit.

Hearing protection: selecting, wearing and maintaining it

  • Wear it the whole time. The Code's example: a protector worn for a full 8-hour day gives the 30 dB maximum protection, but one hour without it drops the protection to 9 dB.
  • Check the supplier's SLC80 rating and class (tested to AS/NZS 1270:2002). Select and maintain protectors under AS/NZS 1269.3:2005, and give workers a choice of types.
  • Earmuffs vs plugs: plugs are hard to fit hygienically with dirty hands, so muffs often suit the workshop better, but muffs can be hot and can clash with helmets. Check fit while wearing the face shield, safety glasses or respirator you actually use.
  • Signpost hearing protector areas and mark the boundaries. Anyone entering, including managers and visitors, should wear protection however briefly they're there. Where signs aren't practical, put warning notices on the tools themselves.
  • Inspect regularly: earmuff seals undamaged, headband tension OK, no home-made modifications, foam plugs soft and clean. Disposable plugs are single use.
  • You must provide training and instruction on the proper use, fit, care and maintenance of the protectors.

Audiometric testing: how to run it

  • Baseline: test new starters and people changing jobs before they're exposed if you can, so later tests have a reference point. It has to happen within 3 months either way.
  • Follow-ups: at least every 2 years, done well into the shift so temporary hearing loss shows up. More often may be needed where LAeq,8h is 100 dB(A) or more.
  • Who: competent persons using AS/NZS 1269.4:2014 (Auditory assessment).
  • Consult first: talk to workers and any HSRs before starting the program so they understand it's there to check the controls are working.
  • Results: give each worker their results with a written explanation. Individual results only go to others with the worker's consent; HSRs can see de-identified and group data.
  • If hearing has shifted or a worker reports new tinnitus, you must review your controls so people don't have to rely on earmuffs and plugs.

Grinders, vibration and solvents make it worse

The Code lists grinders, sanders and drills among the tools that expose workers to both noise and hand-arm vibration, and some chemicals (ototoxic substances, such as certain solvents and metal compounds) can damage hearing too. It recommends regular audiometric testing where workers are exposed to noise above 80 dB(A) LAeq,8h (or 135 dB(C) peak) combined with ototoxic substances or hand-arm vibration at any level, or to listed ototoxic substances above 50% of their exposure limit. Check your SDSs.

Fab shop noise checklist

  1. Walk the shop and run the 1 metre test at each workstation and task.
  2. List every noisy task (grinding, gouging, hammering, press brake, guillotine, compressor, blow-down) and who does it and for how long.
  3. Get a competent person to measure, or use manufacturer data where there's a single source.
  4. Run the ready reckoner per worker, adding +1 dB for 10-hour-plus shifts.
  5. Fix the biggest contributors first with method, isolation and engineering controls.
  6. Where anyone is still over 85 dB(A), signpost the area, issue the right class of protector and train people in fit and care.
  7. Book audiometric tests for those workers (QLD: within 3 months, then at least every 2 years) and diary the next due date.
  8. Run a noise toolbox talk and record attendance, and re-assess whenever plant, layout or work patterns change.

Quick answers

What is the workplace noise exposure standard in Australia?

The exposure standard for noise is an 8-hour equivalent continuous level (LAeq,8h) of 85 dB(A), or a C-weighted peak (LC,peak) of 140 dB(C). In Queensland it is set by section 56 of the Work Health and Safety Regulation 2011, and section 57 says a PCBU must ensure workers aren't exposed above it (maximum penalty 60 penalty units).

How long can I work at 91 or 100 dB(A) without hearing protection?

Every 3 dB increase halves the safe time. The Code of Practice's Table 1 gives 8 hours at 85 dB(A), 4 hours at 88, 2 hours at 91, 1 hour at 94, 30 minutes at 97 and 15 minutes at 100 dB(A) before the standard is exceeded.

Is audiometric testing mandatory in Queensland?

Yes, since 29 July 2025. Under section 58 of the Queensland WHS Regulation 2011, if you require a worker to use hearing protection because their noise exposure exceeds the exposure standard, you must provide audiometric testing within 3 months of the worker starting that work and at least every 2 years. Maximum penalty is 60 penalty units. WorkSafe Queensland's Code of Practice says the PCBU must organise and pay for it.

Who can do a noise assessment?

The Code of Practice says a competent person, following AS/NZS 1269.1:2005. That can be an occupational hygienist, or someone in the business with the skills and experience. Where only one machine is above 85 dB(A) and the manufacturer gives noise data for how it's used, an assessment can sometimes be made without measuring.

What class of hearing protector do I need?

The Code of Practice recommends Class 1 for an LAeq,8h under 90 dB(A), Class 2 for 90 to under 95, Class 3 for 95 to under 100, Class 4 for 100 to under 105 and Class 5 for 105 to under 110. Don't over-protect: too much attenuation makes it hard to hear instructions and warnings.
