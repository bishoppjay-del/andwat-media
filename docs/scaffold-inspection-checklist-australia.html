Scaffold inspection checklist (Australia): the 30-day rule, handover and what to check

Last reviewed 10 November 2026 · Sources linked below · Written by Jay at Andwat Co, an Australian boilermaker/fabricator (Cert III Engineering, 12+ years in the trade). About

When Australian scaffolds must be inspected under WHS regulation 225 (handover, after incidents or repairs, at least every 30 days), what a competent person checks, the records to keep, and QLD specifics. Official sources linked.

Scaffolds go up quickly and then get forgotten. Planks get pulled for another job, a tie comes off so a window can go in, a truck clips a standard, and nobody writes anything down. The WHS rules on scaffold inspection are short, but the paperwork is where most sites fall over. This guide covers what the model WHS Regulations (and Queensland's version) actually require, what a competent person checks, and the records worth keeping. It is general information, not legal or engineering advice, and Victoria has its own OHS Regulations, so check WorkSafe Victoria there.

The short answer

  • Who it applies to: suspended, cantilevered, spur and hung scaffolds, plus any scaffold a person or thing could fall more than 4 m from (WHS regulation 225).
  • Before first use: written confirmation from a competent person that construction is complete, usually a handover certificate.
  • Re-inspect: before use resumes after an incident that could affect stability, after repairs, and at least every 30 days.
  • If it fails: fix it, then have a competent person inspect it again before anyone goes back on.
  • Incomplete or unattended: stop unauthorised access, for example with danger tags and signs.
  • Under 4 m: Safe Work Australia says still inspect before use and after any incident, repair, alteration or addition.

What regulation 225 requires

Regulation 225 sits in the plant chapter of the model WHS Regulations and has been adopted by every state and territory except Victoria. In Queensland it is section 225 of the Work Health and Safety Regulation 2011. The duty falls on the person with management or control of the scaffold at the workplace, usually the principal contractor or builder, not only the scaffolding company.

  1. Written confirmation before use. The scaffold must not be used until a competent person who has inspected it confirms in writing that construction is complete.
  2. Competent-person inspections. The scaffold and its supporting structure (the ground, slab, roof or structure it bears on or is tied to) must be inspected before use resumes after an incident that may reasonably be expected to affect its stability, before use resumes after repairs, and at least every 30 days. Queensland's section 225(3) also lists an inspection before the scaffold is used.
  3. Fix and re-inspect. If an inspection shows a risk to health or safety, the necessary repairs, alterations and additions must be made and a competent person must inspect it again before use resumes.
  4. Keep people off. Unauthorised access must be prevented while the scaffold is incomplete or unattended. Queensland's regulation gives danger tags and warning signs as examples.

In Queensland each of these carries a maximum penalty of 60 penalty units. In December 2024 WorkSafe Queensland reported a construction company fined $40,000 in the Maroochydore Magistrates Court after a scaffold incident on a residential housing site. The site supervisor had inspected the scaffold four days earlier and found deficiencies, but they hadn't been fixed by the time of the incident.

The handover certificate

Safe Work Australia's Guide to scaffold inspection and maintenance says the written confirmation usually takes the form of a handover certificate. Its example (Appendix B) and SafeWork NSW's template record:

  • Site address, scaffold location and the scaffolding contractor
  • Scaffold type, duty class (light, medium or heavy duty), height and number of working platforms
  • The design or drawing it was erected to, and the design registration number for prefabricated scaffolding
  • Name, licence number and signature of the competent person, with the date and time

Issue a new certificate after alterations, repairs or additions, and keep the current one on site until the scaffold is dismantled. A scaff tag at the access point showing the last inspection date is common practice and helps, but the tag alone isn't the written record.

What a competent person checks

Safe Work Australia's example scaffold inspection checklist (Appendix A of the inspection guide) groups the checks under these headings. Use them as the backbone of your own checklist:

  1. Surroundings: power lines and clearances, traffic and plant movement, public protection, exclusion zones, weather and wind exposure.
  2. Supporting structure: whether the ground, slab, awning or roof can carry the load; no undermining from trenches or washouts.
  3. Soleboards and baseplates: in place, level, not sinking, adjustable bases within their extension limits.
  4. Scaffold structure: standards plumb, ledgers and transoms in place, bracing complete, ties and anchors present and tight (ties are the most common thing removed by other trades), couplers secure, no damaged or rusted components.
  5. Working platforms: full width for the duty, planks secured and not overhanging too far, no gaps, guardrails, mid-rails and toeboards fitted, load not over the duty rating.
  6. Access: stair modules or ladders secured, landings and gates working, hatches closed.
  7. Sheeting and containment: shade cloth, mesh or sheeting fixed, and the extra wind load allowed for in the design.
  8. Mobile scaffolds: castors locked, on firm level ground, height-to-base ratio within the manufacturer's limit, nobody on it while it's moved.
  9. General fitness for purpose: still suits the work being done, signs and tags current, handover certificate on site.

SafeWork NSW also recommends a quick visual check by the people using the scaffold every day before use. A daily pre-use check doesn't replace the competent-person inspection, but it catches the missing plank or loose tie between them.

Records to keep

Safe Work Australia says inspection records should be kept at the workplace or readily accessible near the scaffold, and should include the location, the date and time of the inspection, comments, the relevant design or specification reference, and the person who carried it out. A practical register has one row per scaffold and one line per inspection:

  • Scaffold ID, location, type, duty class and height, and whether a person or thing could fall more than 4 m
  • Who erected it and their licence number, and the handover certificate date
  • Each inspection: date, time, inspector, result (safe, defects fixed, not safe and tagged out) and defects found
  • The next inspection due date (no more than 30 days after the last for a regulation 225 scaffold)
  • Repairs and alterations, and the re-inspection after each

Treat any gap of more than 30 days between inspections of a regulation 225 scaffold as a finding in its own right. If something goes wrong, the register is the first thing an inspector will ask for, and it's also worth logging the event in your incident and near-miss register.

Licences, SWMS and Queensland specifics

  • Licence. Scaffolding work where a person or thing could fall more than 4 m is high risk work and needs a scaffolding high risk work licence at the basic, intermediate or advanced level. See the high risk work licence renewal guide for the 5-year expiry rule in Queensland.
  • SWMS. Construction work with a risk of a person falling more than 2 m is high risk construction work and needs a safe work method statement. See SWMS for high risk construction work.
  • Erecting and dismantling in QLD. Section 306P of the Queensland regulation stops a PCBU erecting scaffolding where a person could fall 3 m on housing construction, or 2 m on other construction, unless a control measure prevents the fall, a fall arrest harness system is used, or the specific erection process in section 306P(4) is followed.
  • Engineer inspections in QLD. The Scaffolding Code of Practice 2021 recommends an engineer, not just a scaffolder, for the initial inspection of loading bays over 9 m or 2 tonnes, perimeter demolition scaffolds over 9 m and public access structures. It also calls for two means of access and egress on larger external perimeter scaffolds, other than on detached houses.
  • Standards. Scaffolding components are covered by the AS/NZS 1576 series, and AS 4576:2020 Guidelines for scaffolding replaced AS/NZS 4576:1995 in Australia. Buy Standards from Standards Australia if you need the full text.

Common questions

How often does a scaffold need to be inspected in Australia?

For suspended, cantilevered, spur and hung scaffolds, and any scaffold a person or thing could fall more than 4 m from, WHS regulation 225 requires a competent person to inspect the scaffold and its supporting structure before use resumes after an incident that could affect its stability, before use resumes after repairs, and at least every 30 days. Queensland's regulation also lists an inspection before the scaffold is first used. Safe Work Australia says scaffolds under 4 m should still be inspected before use and after any incident, repair, alteration or addition.

What is a scaffold handover certificate?

It is the usual form of the written confirmation regulation 225 requires: a competent person who has inspected the scaffold confirms its construction is complete before anyone uses it. Safe Work Australia says a new handover certificate should be issued after alterations, repairs or additions, and the certificate should be kept at the workplace until the scaffold is dismantled.

Who can inspect a scaffold?

The regulation says a competent person, meaning someone with the knowledge and skills from training, a qualification or experience to do the inspection. In practice that is usually a licensed scaffolder for scaffolds over 4 m. Queensland's Scaffolding Code of Practice 2021 recommends an engineer for the initial inspection of some higher-risk scaffolds, such as loading bays over 9 m or 2 tonnes, perimeter demolition scaffolds over 9 m and public access structures.

Do I need a licence to put up a scaffold?

Erecting, altering or dismantling a scaffold from which a person or thing could fall more than 4 m is high risk work and needs a scaffolding high risk work licence (basic, intermediate or advanced). Under 4 m the work should still be done by a competent person, and construction work with a risk of falling more than 2 m needs a SWMS.
