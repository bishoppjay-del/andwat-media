You've sent the reminders, the customer has gone quiet, and the invoice is weeks overdue. The next step is a letter of demand: one clear, polite, written demand with a pay-by date and an honest statement of what you'll do next. This page gives you a fill-in letter based on the Queensland Small Business Commissioner's free template and Caxton Legal Centre's sample, plus what happens after it in Queensland. For the full chase sequence (reminders, ASBFEO, debt collectors, GST bad debts) see how to chase an unpaid invoice. It's general information, not legal advice.

The short answer

  • Send it after your reminders fail — business.gov.au lists it as the step after reminders and before dispute help, debt collectors or legal action.
  • Include the agreement, the invoice number, date and amount, a firm pay-by date (usually 14 days), how to pay, and what you'll do if it isn't paid.
  • Send one letter, by registered post or another way you can prove, and keep a copy — it can be used as evidence.
  • Only threaten what you'll actually do. No harassment, and never make it look like a court document.
  • Next step in QLD: a QCAT minor debt application for a fixed or agreed amount up to $25,000 (excluding interest).
  • Construction contract? A letter of demand isn't a substitute for the statutory payment claim route — see the QLD BIF Act payment claim guide.

Why a letter of demand matters

Caxton Legal Centre (Queensland Law Handbook) gives two reasons to send one: it warns the other party you intend to take further action and gives them a last chance to pay, and if the matter goes to a court or tribunal it is evidence you tried to resolve it first. Its advice is that only one letter should be sent and that you should be prepared to follow through on whatever you say will happen. The Queensland Small Business Commissioner's template ends by saying the letter may be tendered to the court or tribunal as evidence — so write it as if a tribunal member will read it, because one might.

What to put in it

Combining the Queensland Small Business Commissioner template and Caxton's sample, a letter of demand should have:

  1. Your details — name, business name, ABN, address and contact details (on letterhead if you have it), and the date.
  2. The debtor's details — the correct person or legal entity and address. Check the ABN or company name on ABN Lookup; QCAT needs the respondent's correct name and address if it goes further.
  3. A clear heading — "Letter of demand – outstanding payment".
  4. What was agreed — the job, the quote or contract, and what went wrong (in one or two factual sentences).
  5. The documents — invoice number, date and amount, with copies enclosed.
  6. Your earlier attempts — the dates of reminders, calls and texts (Caxton's sample includes this).
  7. The demand — full payment of a stated amount by a stated date.
  8. How to pay — bank details or other method.
  9. Optional offer — a discounted settlement or instalments, made "without prejudice" to your right to recover the full debt (this is in the QSBC template).
  10. What happens next — a clear, honest statement, e.g. that you'll start proceedings in QCAT without further notice.
  11. Signature — signed and dated, with your phone and email.

Free letter of demand template (fill in the brackets)

Adapted for tradies from the Queensland Small Business Commissioner template (Creative Commons Attribution 4.0). Delete any optional paragraph you don't want and check every bracket before you send it.

[Your name] trading as [business name]
ABN [your ABN]
[Your address] · [phone] · [email]

[Date]

[Debtor's name]
[Debtor's business name and ABN, if any]
[Debtor's address]

Dear [debtor's name]

Re: Letter of demand – outstanding payment, invoice [number]

I am writing about the [describe the work, e.g. fabrication and installation of a steel carport frame] at [site address], which you accepted on [date] under my quote [number] dated [date]. The work was completed on [date].

I enclose invoice [number] dated [date] for $[amount], which was due on [due date] and remains unpaid. I contacted you about this invoice on [dates and method, e.g. 3 March by email, 12 March by phone], but have not received payment.

I demand full payment of $[amount] by [date — usually 14 days from when the letter is received].

Payment can be made to: [account name], BSB [xxx-xxx], account [number], reference [invoice number].

[Optional] Alternatively, and without prejudice to my right to recover the full debt, I am prepared to accept instalments of $[amount] per [week/month], the first payment on [date], until the debt is paid in full. Please confirm in writing by [date] if you wish to accept this.

If this amount is not paid by the date above, I intend to start proceedings in the Queensland Civil and Administrative Tribunal (QCAT) to recover the debt without further notice to you, and this letter may be given to the tribunal as evidence.

If you wish to discuss this, please contact me on [phone] or at [email].

Yours sincerely
[Signature]
[Your name]

Keep the tone flat and factual. If your quote or terms set a late-payment interest rate, you can state the interest that has accrued under those terms; if they didn't, leave interest out rather than inventing a figure (Queensland construction contracts are the exception: see interest on overdue invoices).

How long to give them, and how to send it

  • Time to pay: the QSBC template says the due date is usually within 14 days from receipt of the letter; Caxton's sample calls a reasonable time normally 14 days. Write an actual date. If you post it, allow for delivery time.
  • Delivery: Caxton advises sending it by registered post (or fax) to confirm receipt and keeping a copy. An emailed PDF as well does no harm — keep the sent email.
  • Record it: add the date sent, method and tracking number to your job file. If you end up at QCAT, you'll want the quote, invoice, reminders, the letter and proof of delivery in one place.

What not to do

  • Don't harass. Caxton warns the debtor can complain to government agencies and the police about harassment. business.gov.au points creditors to the ACCC's debt collection information, and the ACCC and ASIC debt collection guideline applies to creditors who collect their own debts, not just agencies.
  • Don't dress it up as a court document. Caxton says a letter designed to look like a court document is illegal.
  • Don't bluff. Only name the action you'll really take (Caxton: be prepared to act on any consequences you outline).
  • Don't send a string of "final" letters. One letter of demand, then act.
  • Don't overstate the amount. Claim what the invoice says (plus any interest your terms actually provide for), not an inflated figure.

After the deadline: QCAT minor debt or consumer/trader dispute

If the money doesn't arrive, QCAT says a minor debt application can be made by individuals or businesses where the claim:

  • is for no more than $25,000 (excluding interest) — a bigger debt can be reduced to $25,000 to stay in QCAT
  • involves a previous agreement about payment (QCAT lists an unpaid invoice, account or demand)
  • relates to an agreement made no more than 6 years ago

The respondent can live interstate if the agreement was initiated in Queensland. Watch one exception: QCAT's list says work performed or goods supplied at an agreed cost can go in as a minor debt except if the dispute is with a consumer or trader — disputes from a contract between a consumer and a trader (or between two traders) for goods or services up to $25,000 are consumer and trader disputes, a different QCAT application. QCAT registry staff can't tell you which one to file, so get advice if you're unsure.

What happens next, per QCAT:

  • You serve a copy of the application on the respondent, following QCAT's service rules.
  • Claims under $1,500 go straight to a hearing; claims over $1,500 go to mediation first, then a hearing if not resolved.
  • If the respondent doesn't respond (and give you a copy) within 28 days of receiving the application, you can ask QCAT for a decision by default.
  • Bigger debts: up to $150,000 in the Magistrates Court, up to $750,000 in the District Court, above that the Supreme Court.

Winning an order isn't the same as being paid. Legal Aid Queensland notes that if the debtor still won't pay you must apply to enforce the order, and that you have 6 years (extendable up to 12) to enforce a court or QCAT order. Legal Aid Queensland doesn't advise businesses on recovering business debts, so use a private solicitor (Queensland Law Society's Find a Solicitor), LawRight's QCAT self-representation service, or ASBFEO's dispute support.

Worked timeline (made-up job — example only)

A Logan mobile welder (sole trader, not GST-registered) invoices a small transport company $3,850 for trailer repairs on 14-day terms, due 2 February.

  • 3 Feb — friendly reminder email with the invoice re-attached.
  • 12 Feb — phone call, then a second reminder confirming the call.
  • 20 Feb — letter of demand (template above) by registered post plus emailed PDF, demanding payment by 9 March. Tracking number logged.
  • 10 Mar — still unpaid. The welder checks the company's ABN and registered name, then lodges a QCAT minor debt application for $3,850 with the quote, invoice, reminders, letter of demand and delivery record attached, and serves it on the company.
  • Because the claim is over $1,500, QCAT lists it for mediation first.

Common questions

How long should a letter of demand give the customer to pay?

There's no single fixed period for every debt, but the Queensland Small Business Commissioner template says the due date is usually within 14 days from receipt of the letter, and Caxton Legal Centre's sample says a reasonable time is normally 14 days. Put an actual calendar date in the letter so there's no argument about when time is up.

Do I need a lawyer to send a letter of demand?

No. business.gov.au says you can send a letter of demand yourself or ask a legal professional to draft one. A free template is published by the Queensland Small Business Commissioner. Get legal advice if the debt is large, disputed, or owed by a company that may be insolvent.

Can I send a letter of demand by email?

The sources here don't require any particular delivery method for a letter of demand itself. Caxton Legal Centre recommends registered post (or fax) so you can confirm receipt, and keeping a copy. Many tradies also email a PDF copy. Keep proof of how and when it was sent, because the letter can be used as evidence later.

What happens if they ignore the letter of demand?

If the amount is fixed or agreed and no more than $25,000 (excluding interest), you may be able to lodge a QCAT minor debt application in Queensland. You must serve the application on the respondent, and if they don't respond within 28 days of receiving it you can ask QCAT for a decision by default. Larger claims go to the Magistrates Court (up to $150,000) or higher courts.
