Christmas gifts, parties and bonuses for tradies: what the ATO says (2026)

Last reviewed 15 November 2026 · Sources linked below · Written by Jay at Andwat Co, an Australian boilermaker/fabricator (Cert III Engineering, 12+ years in the trade). About

Staff and apprentice gifts under $300 and FBT, why a Christmas party is often not deductible, client hampers vs tickets (TD 2016/14), and cash bonuses under Payday Super.

Every December the same questions come up in the shed: can I claim the slab of beer for the builder, the hamper for a good client, the gift card for the apprentice, or the end-of-year barbecue? The answer depends on who gets it (you, an employee, a client or a subbie) and what it is (a gift, entertainment, or cash). This page sets out what the ATO's own pages and rulings say, so you know which questions to ask your tax agent before you spend.

It's written for sole traders, subbies and small trade and fab businesses. It's general information summarised from the official sources linked below, not tax or financial advice.

The short answer

  • Gifts to yourself or your family aren't business costs. The ATO says a sole trader or partner is not an employee, so benefits you give yourself aren't subject to FBT, and a gift made for personal reasons is private, not deductible.
  • Employees and apprentices: no FBT on a Christmas gift worth less than $300 per person, as long as it would be unreasonable to treat it as a fringe benefit (the minor benefits exemption).
  • Christmas parties: often FBT-free, but the ATO says if the party isn't subject to FBT, you can't claim a tax deduction for it.
  • Clients: a non-entertainment gift (hamper, bottle of wine to take home) given to win repeat work or referrals can be deductible under ATO determination TD 2016/14. Tickets, meals and other entertainment are not.
  • A cash Christmas bonus is wages, not a gift: tax is withheld, and super applies.

Gifts to employees and apprentices

Fringe benefits tax (FBT) applies to benefits you give employees (including apprentices) and their associates, such as partners. On Christmas gifts the ATO is direct: “If you give your employees a Christmas gift, you don't pay FBT if the value of the gift is less than $300 per person and it would be considered unreasonable to treat it as a fringe benefit.”

  • Less than $300 means less than. A gift worth exactly $300 doesn't meet the threshold.
  • It isn't only a dollar test. The ATO looks at how often and how regularly you give similar benefits, their total value, and whether the benefit is really a reward that replaces pay.
  • Separate benefits are tested separately. The ATO's minor benefits page says connected benefits (its example is a meal, a night's accommodation and taxi travel) are each tested against the $300 threshold, while the total still counts toward whether it's reasonable to treat them as exempt.
  • The FBT year runs 1 April to 31 March, not the financial year.

Can you claim the gift as a deduction? That depends on whether it's entertainment. The Tax Institute's summary of the rules puts it this way: a non-entertainment gift to an employee (such as a hamper or gift card) under the $300 minor benefits exemption is not subject to FBT and is tax deductible and GST-creditable, while an entertainment gift (such as movie or sport tickets) is only deductible where the minor benefits exemption doesn't apply. In plain terms: a hamper under $300 is usually the tidy option; tickets under $300 are usually FBT-free but not deductible.

The Christmas party or end-of-year barbecue

The ATO's common entertainment scenarios page covers Christmas parties:

  • Held on a working day, on your business premises, for current employees only: no FBT on the food and drink.
  • Held off your premises, or including employees' partners: no FBT if it's a minor benefit, meaning the cost for each person is less than $300 and it would be unreasonable to treat it as a fringe benefit.
  • Clients attending: there's no FBT on the costs for clients.

The catch for anyone hoping to claim it: “If the Christmas party is not subject to FBT, you can't claim income tax deductions for the cost of the party.” The ATO's worked example, a $125-a-head lunch at work for employees, partners and clients, ends with no FBT, but also no deduction or GST credits for any of it. If you're not sure whether a job site or a shared workshop counts as your “business premises”, ask your tax agent rather than guessing.

Gifts to clients and builders you work for

Gifts to clients sit outside FBT because clients aren't your employees. Whether you can deduct them is covered by ATO Taxation Determination TD 2016/14. It says a business can claim a deduction for a gift to a current or former client if the gift is made for the purpose of producing future assessable income, for example to get repeat work or referrals.

The ATO's own example fits a tradie well: Sally runs a renovation business and gives a bottle of champagne to a client whose renovation was finished in the previous 12 months, expecting it to bring more work or referrals. The gift isn't personal, so she can claim it. In the second example, Bernard gives champagne to his brother, who once bought a $200 statue from him. That gift was made for personal reasons, so there's no deduction.

TD 2016/14 also lists when a client gift is not deductible:

  • it's capital in nature (for example, an expensive item that's an asset rather than a consumable)
  • it's private or domestic, like a present to a mate or relative who happens to be a customer
  • it's entertainment, such as tickets to the footy, concert tickets or taking the client out for a meal
  • it's a bribe to a public or foreign official.

So a hamper or a bottle of wine dropped off for the builder who gave you three jobs this year is the kind of gift the determination describes. Shouting the site manager a long lunch is entertainment.

Subbies and contractors

The ATO's FBT overview lists benefits provided to contractors among the things that are not fringe benefits, so FBT doesn't apply to a gift for a subbie you hire. Whether you can deduct it is a separate question. TD 2016/14 is written about clients, so we haven't found an ATO ruling aimed specifically at gifts to your own subcontractors. Treat it as one to check with your tax agent, and keep the receipt and the reason either way.

Cash Christmas bonuses

A cash bonus to an employee or apprentice is not a gift for tax purposes. The ATO lists a Christmas bonus as salary and wages, and as ordinary time earnings and qualifying earnings for super guarantee. That means:

  • PAYG withholding: use the ATO's Schedule 5 tax table for back payments, commissions, bonuses and similar payments to work out the tax to withhold.
  • Super: the minimum super guarantee is 12% of qualifying earnings.
  • Payday Super timing (from 1 July 2026): super is on time if the fund receives it within 7 business days after you pay the employee. The ATO's own example: a $1,000 Christmas bonus paid on 7 December 2026, separate from the weekly pay, has its super due on the same day as the next regular payday's super, which is 21 December 2026.

Watch the timing over the break. Under Payday Super, a public holiday that applies to a whole state or territory isn't a business day, and Christmas and New Year make that 7 business day window longer in calendar days. Check the ATO's payment deadlines page before you shut the office.

If you're not registered for GST

Plenty of sole traders under the $75,000 GST turnover threshold aren't registered. If that's you, you can't claim GST credits on any of the above. Where a cost is deductible, you claim the full amount you paid, including the GST. If you are registered, the ATO and Tax Institute examples above show GST credits generally follow the deduction: where entertainment isn't deductible, there's no GST credit either. Keep the tax invoice for anything you claim. See Do I need to register for GST?

A quick record-keeping checklist

  • Keep the receipt or tax invoice for every business gift.
  • Write on it who it was for and why, for example “Hamper, J. Smith Builders, repeat client, referrals”. A $90 bottle shop receipt looks personal a year later without that note.
  • Keep business gifts on the business card or account, and family presents on your personal one.
  • For employees, note each gift's value and date so you can show it was under $300 and a one-off.
  • Keep records for 5 years. See how long to keep business records.

Questions people ask

Are Christmas gifts to clients tax deductible in Australia?

Often, yes. ATO Taxation Determination TD 2016/14 says a business can deduct a gift to a current or former client if it is made to produce future income, such as repeat work or referrals. It is not deductible if it is private (for example a gift to a relative for personal reasons), capital, or entertainment such as event tickets or a meal.

Is a Christmas gift to my apprentice or employee subject to FBT?

The ATO says you don't pay FBT on an employee's Christmas gift if its value is less than $300 per person and it would be unreasonable to treat it as a fringe benefit (the minor benefits exemption). A gift of exactly $300 or more doesn't meet the threshold. Whether you can also claim a deduction depends on whether the gift is entertainment.

Is a cash Christmas bonus treated as a gift?

No. The ATO lists a Christmas bonus as salary and wages and as ordinary time earnings and qualifying earnings for super. Under Payday Super (from 1 July 2026) the super on a bonus is due within the normal payday deadlines; the ATO's own example has a 7 December 2026 bonus with super due 21 December 2026.
