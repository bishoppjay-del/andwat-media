Workshop dispatch schedule for small steel shops (AU)

Last reviewed 6 November 2026 · Sources linked below · Written by Jay at Andwat Co, an Australian boilermaker/fabricator (Cert III Engineering, 12+ years in the trade). About

A practical one-page dispatch board for Australian fab shops and boilermakers: why clipboards fail, how to hand jobs to drivers, and how a shortage check before packing saves a return trip. Links the Small-shop Dispatch Planner.

Small steel shops usually don't need a warehouse WMS. They need a clear answer every afternoon: which jobs are ready to go, what's still short, which ute or contractor is taking which load, and what actually left the bay. This page is a simple workshop dispatch schedule you can run on one A4 sheet or a whiteboard. It isn't legal or transport advice, and it doesn't replace a delivery docket your customer or principal asks for.

Why clipboards fail

A clipboard on the welding bay works until three jobs finish on the same afternoon. Typical failure modes in a 2–8 person fab shop:

  • Two versions of the truth. The welder ticks a job done on one sheet; the office has a different order list on the computer; the driver leaves with neither.
  • Shortages discovered at the truck. Base plates, brackets, fasteners or a painted handrail are still on the rack, so the load goes incomplete or someone drives back.
  • No owner for the hand-off. "It's ready" means different things to the fitter (welded), the painter (coated) and the customer (on site with hardware).
  • No closeout. When a site rings about a missing post, nobody can say whether it left the shop, sat on the ute, or was never packed.

Clipboards aren't the enemy. Running three of them with no single board for today's dispatch is.

One-page job board

Keep one board (whiteboard or printed A4) that only covers today and tomorrow. Columns that work for RHS/SHS gates, stairs, frames and small structural jobs:

  • Job / customer / site — short name and suburb or site code
  • Ready? — cut / weld / paint / hardware complete (or N/A)
  • Shortage — blank if clear; otherwise the exact missing item and qty
  • Load — ute 1, ute 2, customer pickup, or courier
  • Driver / time — who is taking it and the planned leave time
  • Actually left — tick + time after the hand-off, not before

Worked example (made-up)

Three jobs on the board at 2pm: (1) Smith stairs — weld done, paint drying, hardware boxed, load ute 1 at 3:30; (2) Gate pair for Jones — weld done, shortage: 4× M12 dynabolts, do not load; (3) Handrail for council depot — complete, customer pickup after 4pm. The shortage row stops Jones going out half-packed. Smith and the handrail can leave without a meeting.

Rules that keep the board honest:

  • Only the person who checked the bay marks Ready.
  • A job with any shortage stays off the load until the shortage cell is cleared.
  • Wipe or archive yesterday's rows each morning so the board stays one page.

Hand-off to drivers

The risky minute is the hand-off from the shop floor to the person driving. Use a short verbal checklist against the board, then write the closeout:

  1. Read the job row aloud (customer, site, piece count).
  2. Confirm the shortage cell is blank.
  3. Confirm hardware, drawings or ITP pack that must travel with the load.
  4. Note vehicle and leave time on the board.
  5. After the ute is loaded, mark Actually left and keep a paper or PDF closeout (photos help for painted or fragile pieces).

A planned dispatch is not proof of delivery. If the site needs a delivery docket, sign-off or photographic evidence, record that separately from the shop board. For QA-heavy structural work, your fabrication ITP or dispatch check (see the fabrication ITP guide) still sits above this daily board.
